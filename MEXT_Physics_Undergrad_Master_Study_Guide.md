## 1 · How the exam works (test-day facts)

- **60 minutes**, 5 numbered questions. Question 1 is five independent short problems spanning the whole syllabus; Questions 2–5 are single-scenario multi-part problems (mechanics, thermodynamics, waves, E&M in some order). Roughly **22–25 answers total → budget ≈ 2.5 min per answer**.
- Answers are **letters (a)–(o)** written on a separate answer sheet. No partial credit. **No calculator.**
- Most answers are **symbolic**; numeric answers use **2 significant figures** and **g = 9.8 m/s²** (given explicitly in 2018).
- Japanese conventions to absorb now: 物理 = Physics; print your full name and **underline the family name**; the first law is used as **Q = ΔU + W (work done BY the gas)**; capacitor plate area is **S**; sound speed is **V** (lowercase v is reserved for object speeds).

## 2 · The ten speed rules (read twice, then live by them)

1. **Dimensional analysis first.** It kills 2–3 options per question before any physics.
2. **Limiting cases** (let m→0, θ→0° or 90°, M→∞, b→a…) eliminate near-miss options instantly.
3. **Answer in your head before reading the options** — the options are engineered around predictable mistakes.
4. In multi-part questions, **part (n) feeds part (n+1)**. A wrong early answer cascades; sanity-check part (1) hard.
5. **2 sig figs:** carry 3 digits through the arithmetic, round once at the end.
6. Memorize: √2≈1.41, √3≈1.73, √5≈2.24, √10≈3.16; triangles **3-4-5** (and its 9-12-15 disguise), 5-12-13.
7. **Energy first, forces second.** Conservation laws answer most MEXT mechanics in two lines.
8. For any **direction question** (current, charge sign), derive it twice: Lenz's law AND F = qv×B. They must agree.
9. **Never integrate.** Every MEXT answer is reachable with algebra, conservation laws, and areas of rectangles/triangles.
10. **Skip and return.** A stuck part rarely blocks the next sub-question — they often re-state what you need.

## Topic 1 — Uniform Acceleration Kinematics & v–t Graphs

**Theory.** Constant acceleration $a$ means the v–t graph is a straight line of slope $a$; **displacement = area under the v–t curve**; the average velocity over any interval is $(v_i+v_f)/2$. When a problem says "accelerates uniformly from rest to $v$ in time $t$", draw the triangle — the answer is its area.

**Key Formulas**
- $v = v_0 + at$ — [$v$: speed, m/s; $a$: acceleration, m/s²; $t$: time, s]
- $x = v_0 t + \frac{1}{2}at^2$ — position from start, m
- $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2ax$ — time-free form
- $x = \frac{(v_0+v)}{2}\,t$ — acceleration-free form (average velocity)

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. List knowns/unknowns. 2. Choose the kinematic equation that omits the variable you neither know nor need. 3. For graphs: slope = acceleration, area = displacement.

#### Problem 2016-UG-Q1-(1) — Distance from a v–t triangle
**Parameters**
- Given: car starts from rest, reaches speed $v$ after time $t$, uniform acceleration.
- Find: distance traveled during $t$.
- Diagram: v–t graph = right triangle, base $t$, height $v$.

**Formulas needed:** $x = \frac{(v_0+v)}{2}t$ (equivalently, triangle area).
**Calculations:** $x = \frac{(0+v)}{2}t = \frac{1}{2}vt$.
**Answer:** **(b)** $\;\tfrac{1}{2}vt$
**Conclusion:** One line via the area — $a$ is never needed. Trap (a) $vt$ assumes constant speed; sanity: with acceleration from rest the distance must be *less* than $vt$. A 20-second problem — bank the time.

## Topic 2 — Projectile Motion (flat ground, cliffs, inclined landing)

**Theory.** Decompose: horizontal motion is uniform ($a_x=0$), vertical is uniform acceleration $-g$. The **time of flight comes from the vertical motion alone**; feed it into the horizontal. Impact angles come from velocity components, never from position. Three standard launches: angled from level ground, horizontal from a height, and off a cliff.

**Key Formulas**
- Level ground, speed $v$ at angle $\theta$: flight time $t_f = \frac{2v\sin\theta}{g}$; range $L = \frac{2v^2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{g} = \frac{v^2\sin 2\theta}{g}$ (max at 45°); apex $H = \frac{v^2\sin^2\theta}{2g}$.
- Horizontal launch from height $h$: $t = \sqrt{2h/g}$; $x = vt$; $v_y = gt = \sqrt{2gh}$; impact angle $\tan\theta = v_y/v_x$.

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Axes at the launch point. 2. Vertical equation → time. 3. Time → horizontal distance. 4. Velocity components → angles/speeds at impact.

#### Problem 2015-UG-Q1-(1) — Range on level ground
**Parameters**
- Given: particle mass $m$ (irrelevant), launch speed $v$, angle $\theta$, gravity $g$.
- Find: horizontal distance $L$ to landing.
- Diagram: standard parabola from ground to ground.

**Formulas needed:** $t_f = \frac{2v\sin\theta}{g}$, then $L = (v\cos\theta)\,t_f$.
**Calculations:** $L = v\cos\theta\cdot\frac{2v\sin\theta}{g} = \frac{2v^2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{g}$.
**Answer:** **(b)** $\;\dfrac{2v^2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{g}$
**Conclusion:** Mass never enters projectile motion. Limit test: $\theta\to 0 \Rightarrow L\to 0$ — options (a)/(c)/(d)/(f) fail it or the $\theta\to90°$ twin. The identity $2\sin\theta\cos\theta=\sin2\theta$ is why 45° maximizes range.

#### Problem 2018-UG-Q1-(1) — Impact angle of a horizontal launch
**Parameters**
- Given: horizontal launch speed $v$ from height $h$; gravity $g$; $\theta$ = impact angle with ground.
- Find: $\tan\theta$.
- Diagram: half-parabola from a ledge of height $h$.

**Formulas needed:** $v_y = \sqrt{2gh}$ (free fall), $v_x = v$ (unchanged), $\tan\theta = v_y/v_x$.
**Calculations:** $\tan\theta = \dfrac{\sqrt{2gh}}{v}$.
**Answer:** **(a)** $\;\dfrac{\sqrt{2gh}}{v}$
**Conclusion:** Dimension check: (m/s)/(m/s) — dimensionless ✓. Limits: $h\to0\Rightarrow\theta\to0$ ✓; $v\to0\Rightarrow\theta\to90°$ ✓. Pitfall: the impact angle uses *velocity* components, not the displacement ratio $h/x$.

## Topic 3 — Newton's Laws & Action–Reaction Pairs

**Theory.** N2 ($\Sigma F = ma$) applies to **each body separately**. N3 pairs are equal, opposite, of the same type, and act on **different** bodies — so they never cancel within one free-body diagram. MEXT's signature N3 setting is friction between stacked blocks: the plank drags the top block *forward* with $f$, and the top block drags the plank *backward* with the same $f$ (fully worked in 2016-Q3, Topic 4). System trick: when all bodies share one acceleration, treat them as a single mass to find $a$, then isolate one body to expose internal forces.

**Key Formulas**
- $\Sigma\vec F = m\vec a$ per body · $\vec F_{A\to B} = -\vec F_{B\to A}$ · weight $mg$ down.

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. One free-body diagram per body. 2. Axes along the motion. 3. N2 per body. 4. Couple the equations with N3 and constraints (same $a$, same string tension).

**Worked variants:** the friction pair is solved start-to-finish in **2016-UG-Q3** (Topic 4); tension chains appear in the Atwood machine (Topic 7, theory only in this set).

## Topic 4 — Friction (static/kinetic, inclines, stacked blocks)

**Theory.** Static friction adjusts itself up to $f_s \le \mu_s N$; sliding starts when demand exceeds supply. Kinetic friction is fixed at $f_k = \mu_k N$ and points opposite the **relative** sliding of the two surfaces — not necessarily opposite the body's motion. On an incline, $N = mg\cos\theta$ and the slide condition is $\tan\theta > \mu$. In stacked blocks the friction from below is the **only** horizontal force on the top block, so it *accelerates* it forward; its N3 reaction decelerates the lower block. The pair vanishes the instant the velocities match.

**Key Formulas**
- $f_s \le \mu_s N$; $\;f_k = \mu_k N$ · incline: slide $\Leftrightarrow \mu < \tan\theta$; sliding acceleration $a = g(\sin\theta - \mu\cos\theta)$
- Heat generated $=$ friction force × **relative** sliding distance.

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Static or kinetic (is there relative sliding *now*)? 2. Direction of $f_k$: opposite this surface's motion relative to the other. 3. $N$ from the perpendicular balance. 4. N2 per body. 5. Find when sliding stops (equal velocities) and switch regimes.

#### Problem 2016-UG-Q1-(4) — Sliding condition on an incline
**Parameters**
- Given: box of mass $m$ on incline angle $\theta$; static coefficient $\mu$.
- Find: condition for the box to slide.

**Formulas needed:** driving force $mg\sin\theta$ vs. max static friction $\mu mg\cos\theta$.
**Calculations:** slides $\Leftrightarrow mg\sin\theta > \mu mg\cos\theta \Leftrightarrow \tan\theta > \mu \Leftrightarrow \mu < \tan\theta$.
**Answer:** **(a)** $\;\mu < \tan\theta$
**Conclusion:** $m$ and $g$ cancel — pure geometry. The critical angle ($\tan\theta_c = \mu$) is the "angle of repose"; memorize the equivalence both ways.

#### Problem 2016-UG-Q3 — Block riding a sliding plank (parts 1–5)
**Parameters (shared)**
- Given: plank P (mass $M$) on a smooth floor; block Q (mass $m$) on top; both at rest; at $t=0$ P receives speed $v_0$ (rightward $= +$); kinetic coefficient $\mu$ between P and Q; velocities become equal at $t = T$.
- Find: forces on Q and on P; $v_P(t)$; $T$; relative sliding distance.
- Diagram: Q rides on P; for $0<t<T$, P moves right faster than Q, so P's surface slides rightward **relative to Q**.

**(1) Force acting on Q**
*Formulas:* $f_k = \mu N$, $N = mg$; direction = direction the surface under Q moves relative to Q $= +$.
*Calculations:* $F_Q = +\mu m g$.
**Answer:** **(a)** $\;\mu mg$
*Conclusion:* friction is the only horizontal force on Q and it pushes Q **forward**. "Friction opposes motion" is wrong as stated — it opposes *relative* motion.

**(2) Force acting on P**
*Formulas:* Newton's third law; smooth floor ⇒ nothing else horizontal.
*Calculations:* $F_P = -\mu m g$.
**Answer:** **(b)** $\;-\mu mg$
*Conclusion:* a textbook action–reaction pair. Note it is $\mu mg$, **not** $\mu Mg$ — the normal force in this pair comes from Q's weight only.

**(3) Velocity of P at time t**
*Formulas:* $a_P = F_P/M = -\mu mg/M$; $v = v_0 + at$.
*Calculations:* $v_P(t) = v_0 - \frac{m}{M}\mu g\,t$.
**Answer:** **(d)** $\;v_0 - \frac{m}{M}\mu g t$
*Conclusion:* deceleration scales with $m/M$. Meanwhile Q obeys $v_Q = \mu g t$ — independent of any mass.

**(4) Time T when the velocities coincide**
*Formulas:* set $v_P = v_Q$.
*Calculations:* $v_0 - \frac{m}{M}\mu gT = \mu gT \Rightarrow v_0 = \mu g T\,\frac{M+m}{M} \Rightarrow T = \frac{M}{M+m}\cdot\frac{v_0}{\mu g}$.
**Answer:** **(g)** $\;\dfrac{M}{M+m}\dfrac{v_0}{\mu g}$
*Conclusion:* faster route — momentum conservation (frictionless floor): $v_f = \frac{Mv_0}{M+m}$, then $T = v_f/(\mu g)$. Same result, half the algebra. Always keep two routes.

**(5) Distance Q slides against P (0 → T)**
*Formulas:* relative speed $v_{rel}(t) = v_P - v_Q = v_0\!\left(1 - \frac{t}{T}\right)$ falls linearly ⇒ relative displacement = triangle area $\frac{1}{2}v_0T$.
*Calculations:* $d = \frac{v_0T}{2} = \dfrac{Mv_0^2}{2(M+m)\mu g}$.
**Answer:** **(f)** $\;\dfrac{Mv_0^2}{2(M+m)\mu g}$
*Conclusion:* energy audit: KE lost $= \frac{1}{2}Mv_0^2 - \frac{1}{2}(M+m)v_f^2 = \frac{Mm\,v_0^2}{2(M+m)}$; dividing by $\mu mg$ returns the same $d$ ✓. "Heat = friction × relative slide" is a recurring MEXT identity — own it.

## Topic 5 — Circular Motion & Centripetal Force

**Theory.** Uniform circular motion at speed $v$, radius $r$ has acceleration $v^2/r$ pointing at the center. "Centripetal force" is **not a new force** — it is the net radial component of real forces (tension, gravity, spring, normal, Coulomb, magnetic…). Identify the radius carefully: it can be a *stretched* spring length, or shrink mid-problem when a string catches a nail.

**Key Formulas**
- $a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = r\omega^2$ · $\;\Sigma F_{radial} = \frac{mv^2}{r}$ · $\;v = r\omega$, $\;T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = \frac{2\pi r}{v}$

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Locate the center and the radius *now*. 2. Sum the radial components of real forces. 3. Set equal to $mv^2/r$. 4. If heights change, get $v$ from energy first.

#### Problem 2018-UG-Q1-(2) — Spring as the centripetal force
**Parameters**
- Given: mass $m$ on a frictionless table; spring constant $k$, natural length $l_0$, extension $\alpha l_0$ ($\alpha>0$); uniform circular motion about the spring's fixed end O.
- Find: speed $v$.
- Diagram: spring from O to the orbiting mass; orbit radius = full stretched length.

**Formulas needed:** spring force $k(\alpha l_0)$; radius $r = (1+\alpha)l_0$; $F = mv^2/r$.
**Calculations:** $k\alpha l_0 = \dfrac{mv^2}{(1+\alpha)l_0} \Rightarrow v^2 = \dfrac{\alpha(1+\alpha)k\,l_0^2}{m} \Rightarrow v = \sqrt{\dfrac{(1+\alpha)\alpha k}{m}}\;l_0$.
**Answer:** **(e)** $\;\sqrt{\dfrac{(1+\alpha)\alpha k}{m}}\,l_0$
**Conclusion:** both classic traps in one line: the **radius** is the stretched length $(1+\alpha)l_0$, while the **force** uses only the extension $\alpha l_0$. Dimensions: $\sqrt{(N/m)/kg}\cdot m =$ m/s ✓.

**Cross-references:** vertical circle with a changing radius → 2014-Q3 (Topic 6); circular motion from a magnetic force → 2016-Q2 (Topic 37); gravitational orbits → 2018-Q3 (Topics 12–13).

## Topic 6 — Pendulum Motion (simple, and with a nail constraint)

**Theory.** Tension does no work, so **energy conservation gives the speed at any height**; the **radial Newton equation gives the tension**. With a nail a distance $b$ below the pivot, the instant the string touches it the radius shrinks from $a$ to $a-b$ and the center jumps to the nail. The string stays taut only while $T \ge 0$: at angle $\theta$ from the *upward* vertical of the current center, $T = \frac{mv^2}{r} - mg\cos\theta$, so slack begins when $v^2 = gr\cos\theta$. Completing a full loop of radius $r$ requires $v_{top}^2 \ge gr$. (Plain small-angle pendulum: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ — mass- and amplitude-independent.)

**Key Formulas**
- $v^2 = 2g\,\Delta h$ (from rest) · lowest point: $T - mg = \frac{mv^2}{r}$ · top of circle: $T + mg = \frac{mv^2}{r}$
- General (θ from the top): $T = \frac{mv^2}{r} - mg\cos\theta$ · slack $\Leftrightarrow v^2 < gr\cos\theta$ · full loop $\Leftrightarrow v_{top}^2 \ge gr$

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Bookkeep **depths below the release level**. 2. Energy → $v^2$ at the point of interest. 3. Radial N2 with the *current* center/radius. 4. Impose $T\ge0$ (or $T=0$ at critical points).

#### Problem 2014-UG-Q3 — Pendulum catching a nail (parts 1–5)
**Parameters (shared)**
- Given: ball $m$ on a string of length $a$ from pivot O; released from rest at A, level with O; a nail at C, a distance $b$ straight below O, with $a/2 < b < a$; B is the lowest point (depth $a$); D is the top of the small circle about C; gravity $g$; no air resistance.
- Find: speed and tension at B; tension at D; taut-string condition; slack speed for $b=a/2$.
- Diagram: big circle (radius $a$, center O) down to B; past B the string wraps the nail → small circle (radius $a-b$, center C); D sits $a-b$ above C, i.e. at depth $2b-a$ below O.

**(1) Speed at B**
*Formulas:* $v^2 = 2g\Delta h$, $\Delta h = a$.
*Calculations:* $v_B = \sqrt{2ga}$.
**Answer:** **(g)** $\;\sqrt{2ga}$
*Conclusion:* the nail is irrelevant until the string is vertical — energy only sees depth.

**(2) Tension just before B**
*Formulas:* radius still $a$ (center O): $T - mg = \frac{mv_B^2}{a}$.
*Calculations:* $T = mg + \frac{m(2ga)}{a} = 3mg$.
**Answer:** **(c)** $\;3mg$
*Conclusion:* the universal "released from the pivot's level" result: **T = 3mg at the bottom**. Memorize it.

**(3) Tension at D (top of the small circle)**
*Formulas:* depth of D below O: $b - (a-b) = 2b-a$ ⇒ $v_D^2 = 2g(2b-a)$; top of circle: $T + mg = \frac{mv_D^2}{a-b}$.
*Calculations:* $T = \dfrac{2mg(2b-a)}{a-b} - mg = \dfrac{\left[\,2(2b-a)-(a-b)\,\right]mg}{a-b} = \dfrac{-3a+5b}{a-b}\,mg$.
**Answer:** **(f)** $\;\dfrac{-3a+5b}{a-b}mg$
*Conclusion:* with $a/2<b<a$ the denominator is positive, so the sign of $(5b-3a)$ decides everything — which is exactly part (4).

**(4) Condition to reach D with the string taut**
*Formulas:* $T \ge 0$ from (3).
*Calculations:* $5b - 3a \ge 0 \Rightarrow a < \frac{5}{3}b$.
**Answer:** **(d)** $\;a < \frac{5}{3}b$
*Conclusion:* equivalently $b > \frac{3}{5}a$. Same physics as the loop-the-loop criterion, dressed for the exam.

**(5) Slack speed when b = a/2**
*Formulas:* radius $r = a/2$; at angle $\theta$ from C's upward vertical: energy $v^2 = 2g\left(b - r\cos\theta\right) = ga(1-\cos\theta)$; slack: $v^2 = gr\cos\theta = \frac{ga}{2}\cos\theta$.
*Calculations:* $ga(1-\cos\theta) = \frac{ga}{2}\cos\theta \Rightarrow \cos\theta = \frac{2}{3}$; then $v^2 = \frac{ga}{2}\cdot\frac{2}{3} = \frac{ga}{3}$, so $v = \sqrt{ga/3}$.
**Answer:** **(j)** $\;\sqrt{\dfrac{ga}{3}}$
*Conclusion:* two equations (energy, $T=0$) in two unknowns ($v$, $\theta$). $\cos\theta = 2/3 > 0$ means the string goes slack *above* C's horizontal — projectile motion follows (the classic continuation question in other years).

## Topic 7 — Atwood Machine **[theory only — appears in non-uploaded MEXT years]**

**Theory.** Two masses $M > m$ over an ideal pulley share one inextensible string ⇒ equal $|a|$, equal $T$.

**Key Formulas**
- $a = \dfrac{(M-m)g}{M+m}$ · $\;T = \dfrac{2Mmg}{M+m}$

**Problem-Solving Framework.** Whole-system N2 ($\,(M+m)a = (M-m)g\,$) for $a$; then single-body N2 for $T$. Checks: $M=m \Rightarrow a=0,\ T=mg$ ✓; $m\to0 \Rightarrow a\to g,\ T\to0$ ✓.

## Topic 8 — Work–Energy Theorem & Conservation of Energy

**Theory.** $W = Fd\cos\phi$; net work $= \Delta KE$. With only conservative forces, $E = KE + PE$ is constant; friction drains $f \times d_{rel}$ as heat; ideal tensions and normal forces do no net work. Two-body recoil systems (a spring between free masses) need **energy and momentum together**: momentum fixes the speed *ratio*, energy fixes the *scale*. Maximum-speed moments occur where acceleration is zero — for springs, at natural length.

**Key Formulas**
- $KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$ · $PE_{grav} = mgh$ · $PE_{spring} = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$ ($x$ from natural length)
- Isolated pair from rest: $Mv_M = mv_m$ (opposite directions) · friction loss $= f\,d_{relative}$

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Is momentum conserved (no external horizontal force)? 2. Is mechanical energy conserved (no friction, no sticking)? 3. Write both. 4. Identify *where* the asked extremum happens (usually natural length / lowest point).

#### Problem 2014-UG-Q1-(1) — Two masses released with a stretched spring
**Parameters**
- Given: masses $M$ and $m$ on a frictionless horizontal table, joined by a spring of constant $k$; released from rest with stretch $\ell$.
- Find: maximum speed of $m$.
- Diagram: M —spring— m on a line; both fly apart as the spring relaxes.

**Formulas needed:** momentum $0 = Mv_M - mv_m$; energy $\frac{1}{2}k\ell^2 = \frac{1}{2}Mv_M^2 + \frac{1}{2}mv_m^2$; speeds peak at natural length.
**Calculations:** $v_M = \frac{m}{M}v_m$; substitute:
$\frac{1}{2}k\ell^2 = \frac{1}{2}v_m^2\!\left(\frac{m^2}{M} + m\right) = \frac{1}{2}v_m^2\,\frac{m(M+m)}{M}
\;\Rightarrow\; v_m = \sqrt{\dfrac{Mk\ell^2}{m(M+m)}}$.
**Answer:** **(c)** $\;\sqrt{\dfrac{Mk\ell^2}{m(M+m)}}$
**Conclusion:** the speeds peak exactly when the spring crosses natural length (force, hence acceleration, flips sign there). Limit $M\to\infty$ recovers the fixed-wall result $\sqrt{k\ell^2/m}$ ✓ — that single limit eliminates four of the six options.

<a id="t09"></a>
## Topic 9 — Coefficient of Restitution & Bouncing Balls **[theory only — appears in non-uploaded years]**

**Theory.** $e = \dfrac{\text{relative separation speed}}{\text{relative approach speed}}$, with $0 \le e \le 1$. Floor bounce: rebound speed $v' = ev$, rebound height $h' = e^2 h$, and after $n$ bounces $h_n = e^{2n}h_0$. $e = 1$: elastic (KE conserved). $e = 0$: perfectly inelastic — the bodies stick (Topic 10).

**Problem-Solving Framework.** For two-body collisions pair the restitution equation with momentum conservation (two equations, two final speeds). For ball–floor, momentum goes to the Earth and only $e$ matters.

<a id="t10"></a>
## Topic 10 — Inelastic Collisions

**Theory.** Momentum is conserved in **every** collision; kinetic energy is **not** (except $e=1$). Perfectly inelastic onto a resting target: $V = \frac{mv}{M+m}$; the KE lost is $\frac{1}{2}\frac{Mm}{M+m}v_{rel}^2$ (the *reduced-mass form* — worth memorizing, it answers loss questions in one line). Friction dragging two bodies to a common velocity (2016-Q3) is a "slow perfectly inelastic collision": identical final-speed formula.

**Key Formulas**
- $p_{before} = p_{after}$ · stick: $V = \dfrac{mv}{M+m}$ · $\Delta KE_{lost} = \dfrac{Mm}{2(M+m)}\,v_{rel}^2$

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Conserve momentum across the instant (external forces have no time to act). 2. Never conserve mechanical energy across a sticking collision. 3. Compute energies before/after separately if the loss is asked.

**Worked variants:** 2015-UG-Q3 parts (2)(3) — solved inside the full problem in Topic 11; 2016-UG-Q3-(4) common velocity (Topic 4).

<a id="t11"></a>
## Topic 11 — Simple Harmonic Motion (spring–mass: period, amplitude, max velocity)

**Theory.** SHM = restoring force proportional to displacement from equilibrium: $F = -kx \Rightarrow \omega = \sqrt{k/m}$, $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$ — independent of amplitude **and of gravity**. A vertical spring merely shifts the equilibrium down by $mg/k$; the oscillation about it is unchanged. Energy: $\frac{1}{2}kA^2 = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 + \frac{1}{2}kx^2$ with **x measured from equilibrium**. $v_{max} = A\omega$ at equilibrium; $a_{max} = A\omega^2$ at the turning points. From an x–t graph: amplitude = peak value, period = repeat time, $v_{max} = 2\pi A/T$.

**Key Formulas**
- $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ · $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$ · $v_{max} = A\omega$ · $a_{max} = A\omega^2$
- $\frac{1}{2}kA^2 = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 + \frac{1}{2}kx^2$ · vertical equilibrium shift $= mg/k$

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Find the equilibrium. 2. Measure displacement from it. 3. Pin the amplitude from energy or a stated extreme point. 4. $T$ uses the **total** oscillating mass. 5. Graphs: read $A$ and $T$; never differentiate by hand.

#### Problem 2016-UG-Q1-(5) — Maximum speed from an x–t graph
**Parameters**
- Given: sinusoidal $x$–$t$ curve, amplitude $A$, completing one full cycle every $2t_0$ (zero crossings at $t_0$ and $2t_0$).
- Find: maximum speed.

**Formulas needed:** $T = 2t_0$; $v_{max} = \dfrac{2\pi A}{T}$.
**Calculations:** $v_{max} = \dfrac{2\pi A}{2t_0} = \dfrac{\pi A}{t_0}$.
**Answer:** **(d)** $\;\dfrac{\pi A}{t_0}$
**Conclusion:** read $T$ off the graph **first**. Trap (f) $A/t_0$ forgets the $2\pi$ of the circular-motion projection; trap (b) halves it by misreading the period.

#### Problem 2015-UG-Q3 — Ball sticks to a hanging spring–mass (parts 1–5)
**Parameters (shared)**
- Given: mass $M$ hangs in equilibrium from a spring $k$; a ball $m$ moving **up** at $v$ strikes and sticks; the pair then performs SHM; gravity $g$. Extra fact for (5): at the **highest** point of the motion the spring is at its **natural length**.
- Find: initial stretch; speed just after sticking; KE lost; period; the ball's KE just before impact.
- Diagram: ceiling — spring — $M$, with $m$ rising into it from below.

**(1) Stretch before the collision**
*Formulas:* equilibrium of $M$: $kx_0 = Mg$.
*Calculations:* $x_0 = \dfrac{Mg}{k}$.
**Answer:** **(e)** $\;\dfrac{Mg}{k}$

**(2) Speed just after sticking**
*Formulas:* momentum across the instant: $mv = (M+m)V$.
*Calculations:* $V = \dfrac{m}{M+m}\,v$.
**Answer:** **(c)** $\;\dfrac{m}{M+m}v$
*Conclusion:* spring and gravity impulses are negligible during the instant — collisions are treated as instantaneous.

**(3) Kinetic energy lost**
*Formulas:* $\Delta KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{1}{2}(M+m)V^2$.
*Calculations:* $= \frac{1}{2}mv^2\left(1 - \frac{m}{M+m}\right) = \dfrac{Mm}{2(M+m)}v^2$.
**Answer:** **(d)** $\;\dfrac{Mm}{2(M+m)}v^2$
*Conclusion:* matches the reduced-mass formula with $v_{rel} = v$ ✓ — next time skip the algebra entirely.

**(4) Period of the SHM**
*Formulas:* $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m_{tot}/k}$.
*Calculations:* $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{M+m}{k}}$.
**Answer:** **(d)** $\;2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{M+m}{k}}$
*Conclusion:* gravity never appears — it only relocates the equilibrium.

**(5) KE of the ball just before impact**
*Formulas:* new equilibrium stretch $x_{eq} = \frac{(M+m)g}{k}$; at the impact instant the displacement above it is $x_1 = x_{eq} - x_0 = \frac{mg}{k}$; "natural length at the top" ⇒ amplitude $A = x_{eq} = \frac{(M+m)g}{k}$; SHM energy: $\frac{1}{2}(M+m)V^2 + \frac{1}{2}kx_1^2 = \frac{1}{2}kA^2$.
*Calculations:*
$\frac{1}{2}(M+m)V^2 = \frac{g^2}{2k}\left[(M+m)^2 - m^2\right] = \frac{g^2}{2k}\,M(M+2m)$
$\Rightarrow V^2 = \dfrac{g^2 M(M+2m)}{k(M+m)}$; with $v = \frac{M+m}{m}V$:
$\frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{(M+m)^2}{m}\,V^2 = \dfrac{M(M+m)(M+2m)g^2}{2km}$.
**Answer:** **(f)** $\;\dfrac{M(M+m)(M+2m)g^2}{2km}$
*Conclusion:* two bookkeeping facts decide everything — displacement is measured from the **new** equilibrium, and "natural length at the top" pins $A = (M+m)g/k$. Pitfall: assuming $A = mg/k$ (that is only the initial offset).

**Cross-references:** 2014-Q1-(1) (Topic 8) is spring **energy without SHM**; 2018-Q1-(2) (Topic 5) is a spring in **circular** motion — don't reach for $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$ by reflex.

## Topic 12 — Escape Velocity & Surface Gravity

**Theory.** Escape = barely reaching infinity with zero speed: $\frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{GMm}{R} = 0$. With surface gravity $g = GM/R^2$ (so $GM = gR^2$), this becomes the exam-ready $v_{esc} = \sqrt{2gR}$. Between planets, $v_{esc} \propto \sqrt{M/R}$.

**Key Formulas**
- $v_{esc} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2GM}{R}} = \sqrt{2gR}$ · $g = \dfrac{GM}{R^2}$ · $U = -\dfrac{GMm}{r}$ (zero at infinity, always negative)

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Energy at surface = energy at infinity (= 0). 2. Swap $GM \leftrightarrow gR^2$ to match the option set. 3. For ratios, cancel constants before any arithmetic.

#### Problem 2014-UG-Q1-(2) — Escape speed in terms of g and R
**Parameters**
- Given: Earth radius $R$, surface gravity $g$; object launched from the surface.
- Find: minimum speed to escape Earth's gravity.

**Formulas needed:** $\frac{1}{2}v^2 = \frac{GM}{R}$; $GM = gR^2$.
**Calculations:** $v = \sqrt{2GM/R} = \sqrt{2gR}$.
**Answer:** **(e)** $\;\sqrt{2gR}$
**Conclusion:** "launched horizontally" is a distractor — escape *energy* is direction-blind (ignoring rotation/atmosphere). Option (a) $\sqrt{gR}$ is the surface circular-orbit speed; escape is $\sqrt{2}$ times it. Know the pair.

#### Problem 2018-UG-Q3-(1) — Mass of the Earth from g and G
**Parameters**
- Given: $g = 9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, $G = 6.67\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N\,m^2/kg^2}$, $R = 6.4\times10^3\ \mathrm{km} = 6.4\times10^6\ \mathrm{m}$.
- Find: Earth's mass $M$.

**Formulas needed:** $M = gR^2/G$.
**Calculations:** $R^2 = 4.10\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{m^2}$; $gR^2 = 9.8 \times 4.10\times10^{13} = 4.01\times10^{14}$; $M = \dfrac{4.01\times10^{14}}{6.67\times10^{-11}} = 6.0\times10^{24}\ \mathrm{kg}$.
**Answer:** **(a)** $\;6.0\times10^{24}\ \mathrm{kg}$
**Conclusion:** convert km → m **first** — the $10^{18}/10^{30}$ options exist purely to punish that slip. Two significant figures throughout.

#### Problem 2018-UG-Q3-(2) — Escape speed of the Earth (numeric)
**Parameters**
- Given: $g$, $R$ as above.
- Find: $v_{esc}$.

**Formulas needed:** $v_{esc} = \sqrt{2gR}$.
**Calculations:** $2gR = 2(9.8)(6.4\times10^6) = 1.25\times10^8$; $v = 1.12\times10^4 \approx 1.1\times10^4\ \mathrm{m/s}$.
**Answer:** **(c)** $\;1.1\times10^4\ \mathrm{m/s}$
**Conclusion:** ≈ 11 km/s — a benchmark to cache. Option (e) $7.9\times10^3$ m/s is the low-orbit speed, the *other* famous number; knowing both makes the distractor flag itself.

#### Problem 2018-UG-Q3-(3) — Jupiter/Earth escape-speed ratio
**Parameters**
- Given: $M_J = 320\,M_E$, $R_J = 11\,R_E$.
- Find: $v_{esc,J}/v_{esc,E}$.

**Formulas needed:** $v_{esc} \propto \sqrt{M/R}$.
**Calculations:** $\sqrt{320/11} = \sqrt{29.1} \approx 5.4$.
**Answer:** **(b)** $\;5.4$
**Conclusion:** pure ratio — $G$, $g$, radii in meters: none needed. Bracket the root: $\sqrt{25}=5 < \sqrt{29.1} < \sqrt{36}=6$, closer to 5.4. Bracketing beats long division under time pressure.

## Topic 13 — Kepler's Third Law & Orbital Mechanics

**Theory.** A circular orbit is uniform circular motion whose centripetal force is gravity:
$\dfrac{GMm}{r^2} = \dfrac{mv^2}{r} = m\omega^2 r$. This yields $v_{orb} = \sqrt{GM/r}$ and Kepler III: $T^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{GM}r^3$ — measure a satellite's $(R, T)$ and you have *weighed the planet*. Geostationary orbit: set $T$ = 1 day. Substitute $GM = gR^2$ whenever surface data is given.

**Key Formulas**
- $v_{orb} = \sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{r}}$ · $T^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2 r^3}{GM}$ · $M = \dfrac{4\pi^2R^3}{GT^2}$ · geostationary: $r = \left(\dfrac{gR^2T^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3}$

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Write gravity $= m\omega^2 r$ (using $\omega = 2\pi/T$ is cleaner than $v$). 2. Solve symbolically for the requested quantity. 3. Numbers last, 2 significant figures.

#### Problem 2015-UG-Q1-(2) — Planet mass from its moon
**Parameters**
- Given: a moon orbits at radius $R$ with period $T$; gravitational constant $G$; circular orbit.
- Find: the planet's mass $M$.

**Formulas needed:** $\dfrac{GMm}{R^2} = m\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{T}\right)^2 R$.
**Calculations:** $M = \dfrac{4\pi^2 R^3}{GT^2}$.
**Answer:** **(c)** $\;\dfrac{4\pi^2R^3}{GT^2}$
**Conclusion:** dimensional analysis alone forces $R^3/(GT^2)$ — with $[G] = \mathrm{N\,m^2/kg^2}$, only that combination has units of kg. A 15-second question if you trust dimensions.

#### Problem 2018-UG-Q3-(4) — Geostationary orbit radius
**Parameters**
- Given: $T = 24\ \mathrm{h} = 8.64\times10^4\ \mathrm{s}$; $g = 9.8$; $R = 6.4\times10^6\ \mathrm{m}$; satellite period = Earth's rotation period.
- Find: orbit radius $r$.

**Formulas needed:** $r^3 = \dfrac{GMT^2}{4\pi^2} = \dfrac{gR^2T^2}{4\pi^2}$.
**Calculations:** $gR^2 = 4.01\times10^{14}$; $T^2 = 7.46\times10^9$; product $= 2.99\times10^{24}$; divide by $4\pi^2 \approx 39.5$ ⇒ $r^3 = 7.6\times10^{22}$; $r = \sqrt[3]{76\times10^{21}} \approx 4.2\times10^7\ \mathrm{m}$.
**Answer:** **(b)** $\;4.2\times10^7\ \mathrm{m}$
**Conclusion:** ≈ 42,000 km from Earth's center (≈ 36,000 km altitude) — another benchmark worth caching. Cube-root technique: rewrite as $n \times 10^{3k}$ so the exponent divides by 3, then root the mantissa ($\sqrt[3]{76} \approx 4.2$ since $4.2^3 \approx 74$).

# PART II — THERMODYNAMICS

<a id="t14"></a>
## Topic 14 — Ideal Gas Law

**Theory.** $PV = nRT$ holds for every equilibrium state. With $n = m/M_{mol}$, a container at **fixed P and V** holds a mass $m \propto 1/T$ — the key to "air escapes a heated room" problems (the room leaks; pressure and volume stay put, molecules leave). For multi-state or multi-container setups, write $PV = nRT$ once per container per moment and take ratios so constants cancel.

**Key Formulas**
- $PV = nRT$ — [$P$: Pa; $V$: m³; $n$: mol; $T$: K]
- Fixed $P,V$: $\;mT = \text{const}$ · density $\rho = \dfrac{PM_{mol}}{RT}$

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Tabulate $(P, V, T, n)$ per state. 2. Identify the invariants (what is held fixed?). 3. Ratio out everything constant; only then insert numbers.

#### Problem 2014-UG-Q1-(3) — Air escaping a warming room
**Parameters**
- Given: room $V = 100\ \mathrm{m^3}$ at constant $P = 1.0\times10^5$ Pa; air density $1.3\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$ at 270 K; room warmed 270 K → 300 K; air leaks so P stays atmospheric.
- Find: mass of air that escapes.

**Formulas needed:** fixed $P, V \Rightarrow mT = \text{const}$.
**Calculations:** $m_1 = \rho V = 1.3 \times 100 = 130$ kg; $m_2 = 130 \times \frac{270}{300} = 117$ kg; escaped $= 130 - 117 = 13$ kg.
**Answer:** **(c)** $\;13$ kg
**Conclusion:** the 0.13 / 1.3 / 13 ladder in the options is a pure decimal-slip test — carry units through. Quick estimate: $\Delta m/m \approx \Delta T/T_{final} = 30/300 = 10\%$ of 130 kg ✓.

<a id="t15"></a>
## Topic 15 — Internal Energy of a Monatomic Ideal Gas

**Theory.** $U = \frac{3}{2}nRT = \frac{3}{2}PV$. The second form is the exam weapon: **no need to know n or T separately** — read $PV$ off the diagram. $U$ is a *state function*: $\Delta U$ depends only on the endpoints, never the path.

**Key Formulas**
- $U = \frac{3}{2}nRT = \frac{3}{2}PV$ · $\Delta U = \frac{3}{2}nR\,\Delta T = \frac{3}{2}\,\Delta(PV)$

**Problem-Solving Framework.** Compute $\Delta(PV)$ between endpoints along *any* convenient path; that is $\frac{2}{3}^{-1}$… simply multiply by $\frac{3}{2}$.

#### Problem 2016-UG-Q1-(2) — ΔU in an isobaric expansion
**Parameters**
- Given: monatomic ideal gas expands $100\ \mathrm{cm^3} \to 200\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ at constant $P = 1.0\times10^5$ Pa.
- Find: change in internal energy.

**Formulas needed:** $\Delta U = \frac{3}{2}P\,\Delta V$ (P constant).
**Calculations:** $\Delta V = 100\ \mathrm{cm^3} = 1.0\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^3}$; $P\Delta V = 10$ J; $\Delta U = 15$ J.
**Answer:** **(c)** $\;15$ J
**Conclusion:** $\mathrm{cm^3 \to m^3}$ is $\times10^{-6}$ — THE classic slip (the 5 / 50 / 100 / 150 J options all bait it). Bonus: here $W = 10$ J and $Q = 25$ J — the universal **3 : 2 : 5 split** ($\Delta U : W : Q$) of monatomic isobaric heating.

<a id="t16"></a>
## Topic 16 — Isobaric Processes

**Theory.** $P$ constant ⇒ $W_{by} = P\,\Delta V$ and (monatomic) $Q = \frac{5}{2}\,\Delta(PV)$, $\Delta U = \frac{3}{2}\,\Delta(PV)$. Charles: $V/T = \text{const}$. A **free, frictionless piston in equilibrium pins the pressure**, so "heated slowly" with a free piston always means isobaric (Topic 25).

**Key Formulas**
- $W = P\Delta V$ · $Q = \frac{5}{2}\Delta(PV)$ · $\Delta U = \frac{3}{2}\Delta(PV)$ · $V/T = \text{const}$

**Worked variants:** 2015-Q4 parts (3)(4) — Topic 25; the horizontal legs of both rectangle cycles — Topic 22.

<a id="t17"></a>
## Topic 17 — Isochoric Processes

**Theory.** $V$ constant ⇒ $W = 0$ and $Q = \Delta U = \frac{3}{2}\,\Delta(PV)$ (monatomic). Gay-Lussac: $P/T = \text{const}$.

**Worked variants:** legs A→B and C→D of 2018-Q4; legs B→C and D→A of 2014-Q4 (both in Topic 22).

<a id="t18"></a>
## Topic 18 — Isothermal Processes **[theory only — appears in non-uploaded years]**

**Theory.** $T$ constant ⇒ $\Delta U = 0$ ⇒ $Q = W$. Quasi-static work $W = nRT\ln(V_f/V_i)$; $PV = \text{const}$ (Boyle). MEXT multiple choice rarely demands the logarithm — what is tested is recognizing $PV = \text{const}$ on a diagram and that **all heat absorbed converts to work**.

<a id="t19"></a>
## Topic 19 — Adiabatic Processes (including free expansion)

**Theory.** $Q = 0$ — but there are two *very* different cases:
- **Quasi-static adiabatic** (slow, pushing a piston): $PV^\gamma = \text{const}$, $TV^{\gamma-1} = \text{const}$, $\gamma = 5/3$ monatomic. The gas **cools on expansion** because $W_{by} > 0$ and $\Delta U = -W_{by}$.
- **Free expansion into vacuum** (membrane bursts): nothing is pushed ⇒ $W = 0$, and $Q = 0$ ⇒ $\Delta U = 0$ ⇒ **T unchanged** (ideal gas) ⇒ pressure simply drops by the volume ratio. $PV^\gamma$ does **not** apply — the process is not quasi-static.

**Key Formulas**
- Quasi-static: $\Delta U = -W_{by}$, $PV^{5/3} = \text{const}$ · Free expansion: $T_f = T_i$, $\;P_f = P_i\dfrac{V_i}{V_f}$

#### Problem 2015-UG-Q1-(3) — Free expansion into vacuum
**Parameters**
- Given: gas at $(P, V)$; the partition to an evacuated volume $V'$ is opened; walls insulated (no heat exchange).
- Find: final pressure.

**Formulas needed:** free expansion ⇒ T constant ⇒ Boyle: $PV = P_f(V + V')$.
**Calculations:** $P_f = \dfrac{V}{V+V'}\,P$.
**Answer:** **(a)** $\;\dfrac{PV}{V+V'}$
**Conclusion:** the word **"vacuum" is the trigger**: no work, no heat, no ΔT. Anyone reflexively applying $PV^{5/3} = \text{const}$ lands on a planted wrong option. Limit: $V' \to 0 \Rightarrow P_f \to P$ ✓.

<a id="t20"></a>
## Topic 20 — First Law of Thermodynamics (sign conventions!)

**Theory.** MEXT uses the Japanese-convention statement: $\;Q = \Delta U + W_{by}\;$ — heat **added to** the gas equals the internal-energy rise plus the work done **by** the gas. Signs: $Q > 0$ into the gas; $W_{by} > 0$ when the gas expands. $\Delta U$ is a state function; $Q$ and $W$ are path functions.

**Problem-Solving Framework.** For any multi-leg process build a small table with columns $Q$, $W$, $\Delta U$ per leg: fill any two, the first law fills the third. Over a closed cycle $\Delta U = 0 \Rightarrow Q_{net} = W_{net}$.

<a id="t21"></a>
## Topic 21 — Molar Specific Heats (C_V, C_P)

**Theory & Key Formulas.** Monatomic: $C_V = \frac{3}{2}R$, $C_P = \frac{5}{2}R$; Mayer: $C_P = C_V + R$; $\gamma = C_P/C_V = \frac{5}{3}$. Heats: isochoric $Q = nC_V\Delta T$, isobaric $Q = nC_P\Delta T$. **The PV shortcut:** $nR\Delta T = \Delta(PV)$, hence
$$Q_V = \tfrac{3}{2}\Delta(PV), \qquad Q_P = \tfrac{5}{2}\Delta(PV)$$
— exactly how the cycle problems below are cracked in seconds, with no n and no T.

<a id="t22"></a>
## Topic 22 — PV Diagrams & Cyclic Processes

**Theory.** Net work per cycle = **enclosed area**: positive if the cycle runs **clockwise** on standard P–V axes, negative if counter-clockwise. Over a full cycle $\Delta U = 0$, so $Q_{net} = W_{net}$. Thermal efficiency $\eta = \dfrac{W_{net}}{Q_{in}}$, where $Q_{in}$ sums **only the heat-absorbing legs** (exhaust legs are not "negative input"). Rectangle cycles have only isobaric/isochoric legs — pure $\Delta(PV)$ arithmetic via Topic 21.

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Tabulate $(P, V)$ — and hence $PV \propto T$ — at the corners. 2. Per leg: $\Delta(PV)$ → $Q$, $W$, $\Delta U$. 3. $W_{net}$ = area with the orientation sign. 4. $\eta$ from positive-Q legs only. 5. Sanity: the Q column must sum to the signed area.

#### Problem 2014-UG-Q4 — Counter-clockwise rectangle cycle (parts 1–5)
**Parameters (shared)**
- Given: 1 mol monatomic ideal gas; A$(V_0, p_0)$ → B$(3V_0, p_0)$ → C$(3V_0, 4p_0)$ → D$(V_0, 4p_0)$ → A. (Right along the bottom, up, left along the top, down: **counter-clockwise**.)
- Find: $T_A/T_C$; work on A→B; the leg of maximum heat absorption and its value; net heat exchanged per cycle.
- Diagram: rectangle of width $2V_0$, height $3p_0$ on P–V axes.

**(1) Temperature ratio T_A : T_C**
*Formulas:* fixed n ⇒ $T \propto PV$.
*Calculations:* $PV|_A = p_0V_0$; $PV|_C = 12\,p_0V_0$ ⇒ $T_A = \frac{1}{12}T_C$.
**Answer:** **(a)** $\;T_A = \frac{1}{12}T_C$

**(2) Work done by the gas on A→B**
*Formulas:* isobaric: $W = P\Delta V$.
*Calculations:* $W = p_0(3V_0 - V_0) = 2p_0V_0$.
**Answer:** **(f)** $\;2p_0V_0$

**(3) Which leg absorbs the most heat?**
*Formulas:* $Q_P = \frac{5}{2}\Delta(PV)$, $Q_V = \frac{3}{2}\Delta(PV)$.
*Calculations (units of $p_0V_0$):* A→B: $\frac{5}{2}(2) = 5$; B→C: $\frac{3}{2}(9) = \frac{27}{2}$; C→D: $\frac{5}{2}(-8) = -20$; D→A: $\frac{3}{2}(-3) = -\frac{9}{2}$. Maximum: **B→C**.
**Answer:** **(b)** $\;$B→C

**(4) Heat absorbed on that leg**
*Calculations:* $Q_{B\to C} = \frac{27}{2}p_0V_0$.
**Answer:** **(g)** $\;\frac{27}{2}p_0V_0$

**(5) Net heat over one full cycle**
*Formulas:* $Q_{net} = W_{net} = \pm$ area; counter-clockwise ⇒ negative.
*Calculations:* area $= (2V_0)(3p_0) = 6p_0V_0$ ⇒ $W_{net} = -6p_0V_0$ ⇒ the gas **emits** $6p_0V_0$. Check by summing the legs: $5 + \frac{27}{2} - 20 - \frac{9}{2} = -6$ ✓.
**Answer:** **(f)** $\;$emits $6p_0V_0$
*Conclusion:* a CCW cycle is refrigerator-like: net work done **on** the gas, net heat **out**. Reading the arrow direction on the figure is worth two of the five marks here — circle it before computing anything.

#### Problem 2018-UG-Q4 — Clockwise rectangle cycle with efficiency (parts 1–4)
**Parameters (shared)**
- Given: 1 mol monatomic gas ($C_V = \frac{3}{2}R$); A$(V_0, P_0)$ →isochoric→ B$(V_0, 4P_0)$ →isobaric→ C$(4V_0, 4P_0)$ →isochoric→ D$(4V_0, P_0)$ →isobaric→ A. **Clockwise.**
- Find: $Q_{AB}$; $Q_{BC}$; net work per cycle; thermal efficiency.

**(1) Heat absorbed A→B (isochoric)**
*Formulas:* $Q_V = \frac{3}{2}\Delta(PV)$.
*Calculations:* $\Delta(PV) = (4P_0 - P_0)V_0 = 3P_0V_0$ ⇒ $Q_{AB} = \frac{9}{2}P_0V_0$.
**Answer:** **(e)** $\;\frac{9}{2}P_0V_0$

**(2) Heat absorbed B→C (isobaric)**
*Formulas:* $Q_P = \frac{5}{2}\Delta(PV)$.
*Calculations:* $\Delta(PV) = 4P_0(4V_0 - V_0) = 12P_0V_0$ ⇒ $Q_{BC} = 30P_0V_0$.
**Answer:** **(c)** $\;30P_0V_0$

**(3) Net work per cycle**
*Formulas:* clockwise ⇒ $W_{net} = +$area.
*Calculations:* $W = (3P_0)(3V_0) = 9P_0V_0$.
**Answer:** **(f)** $\;9P_0V_0$

**(4) Thermal efficiency**
*Formulas:* $\eta = \dfrac{W_{net}}{Q_{in}}$, with $Q_{in}$ = positive-Q legs only (AB and BC here; CD and DA reject heat).
*Calculations:* $Q_{in} = \frac{9}{2} + 30 = \frac{69}{2}\,P_0V_0$; $\;\eta = \dfrac{9}{69/2} = \dfrac{18}{69} = \dfrac{6}{23}$.
**Answer:** **(d)** $\;\dfrac{6}{23}$
*Conclusion:* $\eta \approx 26\%$, well below the Carnot bound between the same temperature extremes ($T_C = 16\,T_A$ ⇒ Carnot $= 15/16$) — a sanity reflex worth running every time. The #1 error is subtracting the exhaust legs inside $Q_{in}$: exhaust is exhaust, not negative input.

## Topic 24 — Gas Mixing / Multi-Container Equilibrium

**Theory.** Rigid, insulated containers joined by a cock: no work leaves ($V_{tot}$ fixed), no heat leaves ⇒ the **two conserved quantities are total moles n and total internal energy U**. Final temperature is the *mole-weighted* energy balance — never the average of the temperatures. Final pressure from $P_f V_{tot} = n_{tot}RT_f$.

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. $n_i = P_iV_i/(RT_i)$ per container. 2. $U_i = \frac{3}{2}P_iV_i$ per container. 3. Conserve $\sum n$ and $\sum U$. 4. $T_f$ from $U_{tot} = \frac{3}{2}n_{tot}RT_f$; $P_f$ from the gas law on the combined volume.

#### Problem 2016-UG-Q4 — Two insulated containers joined by a cock (parts 1–5)
**Parameters (shared)**
- Given: containers A and B, volume $V$ each; A holds monatomic gas at $(P, T)$; B at $(2P, 3T)$; connecting-tube volume negligible; no heat to the surroundings or walls; the cock is opened and equilibrium $(T_f, P_f)$ is reached.
- Find: $n_A$; $n_B/n_A$; $U_B/U_A$; $T_f$; $P_f$.

**(1) Moles in A**
*Calculations:* $n_A = \dfrac{PV}{RT}$.
**Answer:** **(e)** $\;\dfrac{PV}{RT}$

**(2) Mole ratio n_B/n_A**
*Calculations:* $n_B = \dfrac{2PV}{R\cdot3T} = \dfrac{2PV}{3RT}$ ⇒ ratio $= \dfrac{2}{3}$.
**Answer:** **(f)** $\;\dfrac{2}{3}$

**(3) Internal-energy ratio U_B/U_A**
*Formulas:* $U = \frac{3}{2}PV$ — temperatures not needed!
*Calculations:* $U_A = \frac{3}{2}PV$, $U_B = \frac{3}{2}(2P)V = 3PV$ ⇒ ratio $= 2$.
**Answer:** **(c)** $\;2$

**(4) Final temperature**
*Formulas:* conserve $n$ and $U$: $U_{tot} = \frac{3}{2}n_{tot}RT_f$.
*Calculations:* $U_{tot} = \frac{3}{2}PV(1 + 2) = \frac{9}{2}PV$; $n_{tot} = \frac{PV}{RT}\left(1 + \frac{2}{3}\right) = \frac{5}{3}\frac{PV}{RT}$.
$\frac{9}{2}PV = \frac{3}{2}\cdot\frac{5}{3}\frac{PV}{RT}\cdot RT_f = \frac{5}{2}PV\,\frac{T_f}{T} \;\Rightarrow\; T_f = \frac{9}{5}T$.
**Answer:** **(f)** $\;\frac{9}{5}T$

**(5) Final pressure**
*Calculations:* $P_f(2V) = n_{tot}RT_f = \frac{5}{3}\frac{PV}{T}\cdot\frac{9}{5}T = 3PV \;\Rightarrow\; P_f = \frac{3}{2}P$.
**Answer:** **(g)** $\;\frac{3}{2}P$
*Conclusion:* the naive "average the temperatures" gives $2T$ — wrong, because the containers hold different mole counts. Sanity: $T < T_f = 1.8T < 3T$ ✓ and $P < P_f = 1.5P < 2P$ ✓, each weighted toward the bigger reservoir of moles.

## Topic 25 — Piston–Cylinder Systems

**Theory.** A frictionless piston in mechanical equilibrium **pins the gas pressure**: vertical cylinder, gas below ⇒ $P_{gas}S = P_{atm}S + Mg$. Heating "slowly" cannot change that balance, so the process is automatically **isobaric** and Topic 16 takes over. ($S$ = piston area, Japanese notation.)

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Free-body the piston → $P_{gas}$. 2. Gas law for the initial T (or n). 3. Isobaric machinery: $W = P\Delta V = PS\,\Delta h$; $\Delta U = \frac{3}{2}\Delta(PV)$; $Q = \Delta U + W$.

#### Problem 2015-UG-Q4 — Vertical cylinder with a massive piston (parts 1–4)
**Parameters (shared)**
- Given: vertical cylinder, frictionless piston of mass $M$ and area $S$; 1 mol of monatomic ideal gas beneath; atmospheric pressure $p_0$; initial gas pressure $2p_0$ and gas-column height $h$; gas heated slowly until the height is $\frac{3}{2}h$; constants $R$, $g$.
- Find: $M$; the initial temperature; work done by the gas; heat supplied.
- Diagram: atmosphere above, piston, gas column of height $h$ below.

**(1) Piston mass**
*Formulas:* piston equilibrium: $2p_0S = p_0S + Mg$.
*Calculations:* $M = \dfrac{p_0S}{g}$.
**Answer:** **(d)** $\;\dfrac{p_0S}{g}$

**(2) Initial temperature**
*Formulas:* $PV = nRT$, $n = 1$, $V = Sh$.
*Calculations:* $T = \dfrac{2p_0Sh}{R}$.
**Answer:** **(i)** $\;\dfrac{2p_0Sh}{R}$

**(3) Work done by the gas**
*Formulas:* isobaric at $2p_0$ (piston balance is unchanged): $W = P\,\Delta V$.
*Calculations:* $W = 2p_0 \cdot S\cdot\frac{h}{2} = p_0Sh$.
**Answer:** **(c)** $\;p_0Sh$

**(4) Heat supplied**
*Formulas:* $\Delta U = \frac{3}{2}\Delta(PV)$; $Q = \Delta U + W$.
*Calculations:* $\Delta U = \frac{3}{2}\left(2p_0S\cdot\frac{h}{2}\right) = \frac{3}{2}p_0Sh$; $\;Q = \frac{3}{2}p_0Sh + p_0Sh = \frac{5}{2}p_0Sh$.

# PART III — WAVES & SOUND

<a id="t26"></a>
## Topic 26 — Traveling Waves & Sound as a Pressure Wave

**Theory.** $v = f\lambda$, $f = 1/T$. There are **two graph types — never mix them**: a graph vs. **time** at fixed position reveals the period $T$; a **snapshot** vs. position at fixed time reveals the wavelength $\lambda$. Sound is a longitudinal *pressure* wave: **maximum density ⇔ maximum pressure** (peak of $\Delta P$). The displacement wave is 90° out of phase with the pressure wave — a pressure antinode is a displacement node.

**Key Formulas**
- $y = A\sin\!\left[2\pi\!\left(\dfrac{t}{T} - \dfrac{x}{\lambda}\right)\right]$ (wave moving toward $+x$) · $v = f\lambda = \lambda/T$
- Pressure amplitude = peak of the $\Delta P$ axis · peak→adjacent trough = half a period (or half a wavelength)

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Identify which graph you hold. 2. Read $A$ and $T$ (or $\lambda$) directly. 3. Convert with $v = f\lambda$. 4. Density questions → the $\Delta P$ curve, not the displacement curve.

#### Problem 2018-UG-Q5 — Sound wave read from a ΔP–t graph and a snapshot (parts 1–4)
**Parameters (shared)**
- Given: sound travels toward $+x$ at speed $v$; at a fixed point, $\Delta P(t)$ is sinusoidal with maximum $+A$ at $t_1$ and the next minimum $-A$ at $t_2$; a later $\Delta P(x)$ snapshot marks axis points a, b, c, d, with the curve at its maximum at d.
- Find: pressure amplitude; period; wavelength; the highest-density point on the snapshot.

**(1) Amplitude of the pressure variation**
*Calculations:* peak value of the curve $= A$.
**Answer:** **(c)** $\;A$

**(2) Period**
*Formulas:* maximum → next minimum spans **half** a cycle.
*Calculations:* $\frac{T}{2} = t_2 - t_1 \Rightarrow T = 2(t_2 - t_1)$.
**Answer:** **(c)** $\;2(t_2 - t_1)$

**(3) Wavelength**
*Formulas:* $\lambda = vT$.
*Calculations:* $\lambda = 2v(t_2 - t_1)$.
**Answer:** **(a)** $\;2v(t_2 - t_1)$
*Conclusion:* (2)→(3) chain — a period error doubles through. Always re-derive (2) before trusting (3).

**(4) Point of highest air density**
*Formulas:* highest density ⇔ maximum $\Delta P$ on the snapshot.
*Calculations:* the curve peaks at point d.
**Answer:** **(d)** $\;$point d
*Conclusion:* compressions live at pressure **maxima**. If a year instead shows the *displacement* snapshot, the compression sits where displacement crosses zero with negative slope (for $+x$ travel) — know both pictures so neither version surprises you.

## Topic 27 — Standing Waves & Superposition

**Theory.** Two identical counter-propagating waves superpose into a standing wave: adjacent **nodes are λ/2 apart**, antinodes midway. In sound tubes, the air **density/pressure oscillates most strongly at displacement nodes** (the closed end / piston face) and not at all at displacement antinodes (the open end). Resonance = the source frequency matches a normal mode of the cavity.

**Key Formulas**
- Node spacing $= \lambda/2$ · pressure antinode ↔ displacement node

**Worked variant:** the density-maximum location inside 2015-Q5-(3), Topic 28 below.

## Topic 28 — Sound Resonance in Pipes (open–closed)

**Theory.** Closed end (piston) = displacement node; open end ≈ displacement antinode. An open–closed column of length $L$ resonates when $L = (2k-1)\dfrac{\lambda}{4}$, $k = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ — odd quarter-wavelengths only — giving frequencies $f_k = (2k-1)\dfrac{V}{4L}$ (**odd harmonics only**). Successive resonance *positions* at fixed frequency are spaced $\lambda/2$, which conveniently cancels the end correction. (Open–open pipes: $L = k\lambda/2$, all harmonics.)

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Sketch the quarter-wave ladder. 2. First resonance fixes $\lambda$ (or $L$). 3. Use the $\lambda/2$ spacing between resonances as the robust measurement. 4. $V = f\lambda$.

#### Problem 2015-UG-Q5 — Piston-tube resonance (parts 1–5)
**Parameters (shared)**
- Given: horizontal tube, open at the left where a speaker of frequency $f$ plays; a movable piston closes it at distance $x$ from the opening; loud sound (resonance) occurs first at $x = l$ and next at $x = 3l$.
- Find: $\lambda$; the speed of sound; the interior point of maximum density variation at the second resonance; how $l$ shifts on a hot day; the next resonance frequency with the piston fixed at $3l$.
- Diagram: speaker | ←— air column of length $x$ —→ | piston.

**(1) Wavelength**
*Formulas:* open–closed ladder: $l = \frac{\lambda}{4}$ and $3l = \frac{3\lambda}{4}$ — consistent.
*Calculations:* $\lambda = 4l$.
**Answer:** **(i)** $\;4l$

**(2) Speed of sound**
*Formulas:* $V = f\lambda$.
*Calculations:* $V = 4fl$.
**Answer:** **(j)** $\;4fl$
*Conclusion:* cross-check: resonance spacing $3l - l = 2l$ must equal $\lambda/2$ ✓ — in lab-style questions use the spacing as the primary measurement (end corrections cancel).

**(3) Maximum density variation at the second resonance**
*Formulas:* density variation is maximal at **displacement nodes**: the piston face $x = 3l$ and every $\lambda/2 = 2l$ toward the opening.
*Calculations:* interior node at $3l - 2l = l$.
**Answer:** **(b)** $\;x = l$
*Conclusion:* the open end ($x=0$) is an antinode — zero density variation. Nodes carry the pressure action.

**(4) Effect of higher air temperature on l**
*Formulas:* $V$ increases with temperature ⇒ $\lambda = V/f$ increases ⇒ $l = \lambda/4$ increases.
**Answer:** **(a)** $\;l$ increases
*Conclusion:* qualitative chain $T\uparrow \Rightarrow V\uparrow \Rightarrow \lambda\uparrow \Rightarrow l\uparrow$ — MEXT loves this one-step inference.

**(5) Next resonance frequency, piston fixed at L = 3l**
*Formulas:* $f_k = (2k-1)\dfrac{V}{4L} = (2k-1)\dfrac{4fl}{12l} = \dfrac{2k-1}{3}f$.
*Calculations:* modes: $\frac{f}{3}, f, \frac{5f}{3}, \dots$ — the current resonance is $f$ ($k=2$); the next above it is $\frac{5}{3}f$.
**Answer:** **(g)** $\;\frac{5}{3}f$
*Conclusion:* only **odd** multiples of $f/3$ exist in an open–closed tube — the step from $f$ is $\frac{2}{3}f$, not $\frac{1}{3}f$, because the even mode does not exist.

## Topic 30 — Doppler Effect (moving source/observer/wall, angled motion)

**Theory.** With sound speed $V$, source speed $v_s$ and observer speed $v_o$ **along the line of sight**:
$$f' = f\,\frac{V \pm v_o}{V \mp v_s} \qquad \text{(upper signs for approach)}$$
Mnemonic: **observer motion edits the numerator** (how fast wavefronts are met); **source motion edits the denominator** (the wavelength itself is changed). A reflecting **wall is a two-step device**: it first *hears* as an observer, then *re-emits* that frequency as a source. Only the **radial** velocity component shifts pitch — resolve angled motion first.

**Key Formulas**
- Source toward static observer: $f\dfrac{V}{V - v_s}$ · away: $f\dfrac{V}{V + v_s}$
- Observer toward static source: $f\dfrac{V + v_o}{V}$ · away: $f\dfrac{V - v_o}{V}$
- Wall: compose (receive as observer) × (re-emit as source)

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Draw the line of sight. 2. Project every velocity onto it. 3. Apply the formula leg by leg (source→wall, wall→listener). 4. For beats, subtract the two received frequencies.

#### Problem 2014-UG-Q5 — Speaker between a wall and a listener (parts 1–5)
**Parameters (shared)**
- Given (left→right): wall D with rider C on its front face — B, a speaker of frequency $f$ — A, an observer; sound speed $V$. Parts (1)–(3): B moves **right** at $v$ (toward A, away from D); everything else static. Parts (4)–(5): B and A static; D and C move **right** at $v$ (toward B).
- Find: direct frequency at A; wall-reflected frequency at A; beat rate at A; frequency heard at C; reflected frequency at A with the wall moving.

**(1) Direct sound at A (source approaching)**
*Calculations:* $f_1 = f\,\dfrac{V}{V - v}$.
**Answer:** **(d)** $\;\dfrac{V}{V-v}f$

**(2) Wall-reflected sound at A (source receding from the wall)**
*Formulas:* static wall receives $f\frac{V}{V+v}$ and re-emits it unchanged; A is static.
*Calculations:* $f_2 = f\,\dfrac{V}{V + v}$.
**Answer:** **(c)** $\;\dfrac{V}{V+v}f$

**(3) Beat frequency at A**
*Formulas:* $f_{beat} = f_1 - f_2$.
*Calculations:* $fV\!\left(\dfrac{1}{V-v} - \dfrac{1}{V+v}\right) = \dfrac{2fVv}{V^2 - v^2}$.
**Answer:** **(g)** $\;\dfrac{2Vvf}{V^2 - v^2}$
*Conclusion:* for $v \ll V$ this is $\approx 2fv/V$ — the standard first-order beat estimate (see Topic 31).

**(4) Frequency at C (observer approaching a static source)**
*Calculations:* $f_C = f\,\dfrac{V + v}{V}$.
**Answer:** **(a)** $\;\dfrac{V+v}{V}f$

**(5) Reflected frequency at A with the wall moving toward B**
*Formulas:* wall as observer (approaching): receives $f\frac{V+v}{V}$; wall as source (approaching A): multiply by $\frac{V}{V-v}$.
*Calculations:* $f' = f\,\dfrac{V+v}{V}\cdot\dfrac{V}{V-v} = f\,\dfrac{V+v}{V-v}$.
**Answer:** **(e)** $\;\dfrac{V+v}{V-v}f$
*Conclusion:* the $V$'s always cancel in the wall composition — a moving-mirror reflection is **always** $\frac{V+v}{V-v}$ on approach. Sanity sweep: every "toward" configuration must *raise* the pitch; all five answers comply.

#### Problem 2018-UG-Q1-(5) — Doppler with angled motion (numeric)
**Parameters**
- Given: source frequency $f = 660$ Hz; sound speed $V = 330$ m/s; observer walks at 5 m/s along the ground, directly away from the point O below the source; the source S sits 9 m horizontally and 12 m vertically from the observer's position.
- Find: observed frequency, 2 significant figures.

**Formulas needed:** distance SO via right triangle; radial speed = (speed) × (horizontal leg / hypotenuse); $f' = f\dfrac{V - v_r}{V}$ (receding observer).
**Calculations:** hypotenuse: $9$–$12$–$15$ (a 3-4-5 triangle ×3) ⇒ 15 m; $v_r = 5 \times \frac{9}{15} = 3$ m/s; $f' = 660 \times \frac{330 - 3}{330} = 2 \times 327 = 654$ Hz.
**Answer:** **(c)** $\;654$ Hz
**Conclusion:** only the **line-of-sight component** (3 m/s) shifts the pitch — plugging the full 5 m/s gives 650 Hz, option (a), the planted trap. Spot 9-12-15 instantly, and note $660/330 = 2$ turns the whole computation into one multiplication.

# PART IV — ELECTRICITY & MAGNETISM

<a id="t32"></a>
## Topic 32 — Coulomb's Law, Field, and Electrostatic Potential Energy

**Theory.** $F = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{q_1q_2}{r^2}$ (repulsive for like signs); point-charge field $E = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{Q}{r^2}$; potential **energy** $U = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{q_1q_2}{r}$ (positive for like charges, zero at infinity). Energy conservation with $U(r)$ solves every "closest approach" problem: approaching a **fixed** charge head-on, the mover is momentarily at rest at minimum separation. (If both charges are free, they share a common velocity at closest approach — add momentum conservation.)

**Key Formulas**
- $F = \dfrac{kq_1q_2}{r^2}$, $\;U = \dfrac{kq_1q_2}{r}$, with $k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \approx 9.0\times10^9\ \mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}$

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Conserve $E = KE + U$ between "far" and "near". 2. Fixed target ⇒ $v = 0$ at closest approach. 3. Solve for $r$; test limits.

#### Problem 2015-UG-Q1-(4) — Distance of closest approach
**Parameters**
- Given: charge $q$ (mass $m$) moving at speed $v$ when at distance $L$ from a **fixed** charge $Q$; both positive; permittivity $\varepsilon_0$.
- Find: minimum separation $d$.

**Formulas needed:** $\dfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + \dfrac{qQ}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 L} = 0 + \dfrac{qQ}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 d}$.
**Calculations:** $\dfrac{qQ}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 d} = \dfrac{qQ + 2\pi\varepsilon_0 mv^2L}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 L} \;\Rightarrow\; d = \dfrac{qQL}{qQ + 2\pi\varepsilon_0 mv^2 L}$.
**Answer:** **(c)** $\;\dfrac{qQL}{qQ + 2\pi\varepsilon_0 mv^2L}$
**Conclusion:** limits nail it instantly: $v \to 0 \Rightarrow d \to L$ ✓; $v \to \infty \Rightarrow d \to 0$ ✓ — most other options fail one of the two. The **$2\pi$** (not $4\pi$) is the fingerprint of the correct option: the $\frac{1}{2}$ from kinetic energy merged into it.

## Topic 33 — Electric Potential & Uniform-Field Dynamics

**Theory.** $V = kQ/r$ is a scalar — just add contributions. A charge crossing a potential difference $U$ gains $\Delta KE = qU$. Between parallel plates the field is uniform, $E = V/d$, the force is $F = qE$, and the rest is **pure kinematics with $a = qE/m$** — projectile motion with gravity replaced.

**Key Formulas**
- $V = \dfrac{kQ}{r}$ · $E = \dfrac{V}{d}$ · $F = qE$ · $a = \dfrac{qE}{m}$ · from rest: $d = \frac{1}{2}at^2$, $\;v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2qEd}{m}}$

#### Problem 2018-UG-Q1-(3) — Time to cross a distance in a uniform field
**Parameters**
- Given: charge $q$, mass $m$, released from rest in a uniform field $E$.
- Find: time $t$ to travel a distance $d$.

**Formulas needed:** $a = \dfrac{qE}{m}$; $d = \dfrac{1}{2}at^2$.
**Calculations:** $t = \sqrt{\dfrac{2d}{a}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2md}{qE}}$.
**Answer:** **(f)** $\;\sqrt{\dfrac{2md}{qE}}$
**Conclusion:** identical to dropping a mass with $g \to qE/m$. The trap option differs only by a factor-2 slip inside the root — write the kinematics line *before* looking at the options.

## Topic 34 — Capacitors: Parallel-Plate Physics & Networks

**Theory.** $Q = CV$; parallel plate $C = \dfrac{\varepsilon_0 S}{d}$ ($S$ = plate area, Japanese notation); the field inside is uniform: $E = \dfrac{V}{d} = \dfrac{Q}{Cd}$. **Identify the constraint first**: a capacitor *disconnected* from its battery keeps $Q$ fixed; one *still connected* keeps $V$ fixed. In a DC circuit "after a sufficient time", capacitors carry no current — analyze by voltages. Combinations: **parallel adds C** (same $V$); **series adds reciprocals** (same $Q$ on each).

**Key Formulas**
- $C_{par} = C_1 + C_2$ · series pair: $\dfrac{C_1C_2}{C_1+C_2}$ · energy $\dfrac{1}{2}CV^2 = \dfrac{Q^2}{2C}$

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Reduce the network from the outside in. 2. Push total charge/voltage back through the reduction tree (series branches share $Q$; parallel branches share $V$). 3. Stop at the element asked.

#### Problem 2016-UG-Q1-(3) — Field inside an isolated charged capacitor
**Parameters**
- Given: capacitance $C$, plate separation $d$, charge $Q$; battery disconnected.
- Find: electric field between the plates.

**Formulas needed:** $V = Q/C$; $E = V/d$.
**Calculations:** $E = \dfrac{Q}{Cd}$.
**Answer:** **(f)** $\;\dfrac{Q}{Cd}$
**Conclusion:** disconnection froze $Q$ — irrelevant here, decisive the moment plates move (then $E = Q/\varepsilon_0S$ is *separation-independent*). Units: $\mathrm{C/(F\cdot m)} = \mathrm{V/m}$ ✓.

#### Problem 2014-UG-Q1-(4) — Charge on C₁ in a battery network
**Parameters**
- Given: battery EMF $E$ with switch closed long ago; $C_1$ parallel $C_2$, that pair in series with $C_3$ across the battery.
- Find: charge stored on $C_1$.

**Formulas needed:** $C_{12} = C_1 + C_2$; series voltage division; $Q_1 = C_1V_{12}$.
**Calculations:** total $C = \dfrac{C_{12}C_3}{C_{12} + C_3}$; the series pair shares one charge, so $V_{12} = \dfrac{E\,C_3}{C_1 + C_2 + C_3}$; then $Q_1 = \dfrac{C_1C_3}{C_1 + C_2 + C_3}E$.
**Answer:** **(b)** $\;\dfrac{C_1C_3}{C_1+C_2+C_3}E$
**Conclusion:** "after a sufficient period" = steady state, zero current, pure voltage bookkeeping. Limits: $C_3 \to \infty \Rightarrow V_{12} \to E,\ Q_1 \to C_1E$ ✓; $C_3 \to 0 \Rightarrow Q_1 \to 0$ ✓ — the two limits uniquely select (b).

<a id="t35"></a>
## Topic 35 — Hall Effect

**Theory.** A conducting strip (thickness $a$, width $b$, length $c$) carries current $I$ via $n$ carriers per volume, charge magnitude $q$, drifting at $v$. **Box counting:** in time $t$, the carriers crossing a section fill a box of volume $(ab)(vt)$ ⇒ $N = nabvt$ ⇒ $I = qnabv$. Apply $B$ perpendicular to the broad face: the magnetic force $qvB$ shovels carriers sideways until the built-up transverse field balances it: $qE = qvB \Rightarrow E = vB$; the Hall voltage spans the width **along the force**: $V_H = Eb = vBb$. Eliminating $v$ measures the carrier density — and the polarity of $V_H$ reveals the carrier **sign**: that is the Hall effect's whole point.

**Key Formulas**
- $I = qnabv$ ($ab$ = cross-section ⊥ current) · $E = vB$ · $V_H = vBb$ · $n = \dfrac{BI}{qaV_H}$

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Box-count for $N$ and $I$. 2. Transverse force balance for $E$. 3. Multiply by the dimension the voltage spans. 4. Eliminate $v$ between the two results.

#### Problem 2014-UG-Q2 — Hall strip, the full chain (parts 1–5)
**Parameters (shared)**

**(1) Carriers crossing a section in time t**
*Formulas:* swept volume $= (ab)(vt)$.
*Calculations:* $N = nabvt$.
**Answer:** **(d)** $\;nabvt$

**(2) Current**
*Calculations:* $I = \dfrac{Nq}{t} = qnabv$.
**Answer:** **(b)** $\;qnabv$

**(3) Magnetic force on each carrier**
*Formulas:* $v \perp B$ ⇒ $F = qvB$.
**Answer:** **(e)** $\;qvB$

**(4) Hall voltage across the width**

## Topic 36 — Lorentz Force

**Theory.** $\vec F = q\vec v\times\vec B$, magnitude $qvB\sin\phi$. It is always perpendicular to $\vec v$, so it **does no work**: speed is constant, only direction changes. For negative charges, reverse the right-hand result. With both fields: $\vec F = q(\vec E + \vec v\times\vec B)$ — the balance behind the Hall effect (Topic 35).

**Framework.** Right hand: fingers along $\vec v$, curl toward $\vec B$, thumb gives $\vec F$ for positive $q$. Worked through in Topic 37's problem.

## Topic 37 — Circular Motion of a Charge in a Magnetic Field

**Theory.** Uniform $B \perp v$ bends the path into a circle: $qvB = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$ ⇒ $r = \dfrac{mv}{qB}$, with period $T = \dfrac{2\pi m}{qB}$ — **independent of speed** (the cyclotron principle). The charge's sign is read from the *first bend* via $\vec F = q\vec v\times\vec B$.

**Key Formulas**
- $r = \dfrac{mv}{qB}$ · $T = \dfrac{2\pi m}{qB}$ · $f_c = \dfrac{qB}{2\pi m}$

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Sign from the first bend. 2. $r$ and $T$ from the boxed formulas. 3. Parameter changes are **ratios** — never recompute from scratch.

## Topic 38 — Electromagnetic Induction (motional EMF, Lenz's law)

**Theory.** Flux $\Phi = B_\perp A$; EMF $= \dfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$; a rod of length $l$ sweeping perpendicular to a field component gives $\mathcal{E} = B_\perp l v$. **The projection rule that decides marks:** only the field component **perpendicular to the circuit plane** makes flux — if $B$ is vertical but the circuit lies on an incline of angle $\theta$, use $B\cos\theta$. Lenz: the induced current opposes the *change* in flux. The force back on the induced current is $F = BIl$ — and if $B$ is vertical that force is **horizontal**, so its along-incline component carries a **second** $\cos\theta$. Terminal velocity: driving force = magnetic brake. Energy: at terminal speed, gravity's power = Joule heating (Topic 42).

**Key Formulas**
- $\mathcal{E} = \dfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t} = B_\perp l v$ · $I = \mathcal{E}/R$ · $F = BIl$ (project onto the motion as needed)
- Coil entering a field region: $\Phi = Blvt$ ⇒ $\mathcal{E} = Blv$, constant during entry

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Define the circuit plane and its normal. 2. Project $B$ onto that normal. 3. EMF → $I = \mathcal{E}/R$. 4. Direction by Lenz **and** by $F = q\vec v\times\vec B$ on the carriers — they must agree. 5. Force on the conductor → dynamics. 6. Close the books with an energy audit.

#### Problem 2015-UG-Q2 — Bar on inclined rails, VERTICAL field (parts 1–5)
**Parameters (shared)**
- Given: conducting bar of mass $m$ on frictionless rails (separation $d$) laid on an incline of angle $\theta$; resistance $R$ across the top; uniform $B$ pointing **vertically up** (perpendicular to the *ground*, not to the incline); the bar is released, slides down, and eventually reaches terminal speed $u$.
- Find: current direction in the bar (a→b or b→a); $I(v)$; the braking-force component along the incline; $u$; gravity's power at terminal speed.
- Diagram: the circuit plane *is* the incline ⇒ flux uses $B\cos\theta$; the force on the bar ($I\vec l\times\vec B$ with vertical $B$) is **horizontal**, so its along-slope component costs a second $\cos\theta$.

**(1) Direction of the induced current**
*Two independent derivations (use both for any direction question):*
- *Lorentz:* positive carriers in the bar move with the bar; the velocity component ∥ $B$ does nothing, the horizontal component crossed with vertical $B$ pushes carriers along the bar toward end a.
- *Lenz:* sliding down grows the enclosed area, so the upward flux $BA\cos\theta$ grows; the induced current circulates to oppose it — through the bar from b to a.
**Answer:** **(b)** $\;$from b to a
*Conclusion:* direction letters depend on the printed figure's labeling — **re-derive on your own copy with this two-route method**; the method is what earns the mark either way.

**(2) Current as a function of speed v**
*Formulas:* $\mathcal{E} = (B\cos\theta)\,d\,v$.
*Calculations:* $I = \dfrac{vdB\cos\theta}{R}$.
**Answer:** **(f)** $\;\dfrac{vdB\cos\theta}{R}$

**(3) Braking force along the incline**
*Formulas:* force on the bar $F = BId$ — horizontal; its along-slope component is $F\cos\theta$.
*Calculations:* $F_\parallel = \dfrac{vd^2B^2\cos^2\theta}{R}$, directed up-slope.
**Answer:** **(c)** $\;\dfrac{vd^2B^2\cos^2\theta}{R}$
*Conclusion:* **two** $\cos\theta$ projections — flux first, force second. This is the single most-missed detail in the whole problem family. (If $B$ were instead perpendicular to the *incline surface*, both cosines disappear.)

**(4) Terminal speed**
*Formulas:* terminal ⇔ $mg\sin\theta = F_\parallel(u)$.
*Calculations:* $mg\sin\theta = \dfrac{ud^2B^2\cos^2\theta}{R} \;\Rightarrow\; u = \dfrac{mgR\sin\theta}{d^2B^2\cos^2\theta}$.
**Answer:** **(e)** $\;\dfrac{mgR\sin\theta}{d^2B^2\cos^2\theta}$

**(5) Power of gravity at terminal speed**
*Formulas:* $P = (mg\sin\theta)\,u$.
*Calculations:* $P = \dfrac{m^2g^2R\sin^2\theta}{d^2B^2\cos^2\theta} = \dfrac{m^2g^2R\tan^2\theta}{d^2B^2}$.
**Answer:** **(e)** $\;\dfrac{m^2g^2R\tan^2\theta}{d^2B^2}$
*Conclusion:* energy audit: at terminal speed $P_{gravity} = I^2R = \left(\dfrac{udB\cos\theta}{R}\right)^{\!2}R$ — substitute $u$ and it matches ✓. That identity can replace part (5) entirely: nature balances the books, so should you.

#### Problem 2018-UG-Q2 — Square coil entering a field region (parts 1–5)
**Parameters (shared)**
- Given: square coil of side $l$, total resistance $R$, moving right at constant $v$; uniform $B$ out of the page fills the region beyond line XY; the leading side enters at $t = 0$; consider $0 < t < l/v$ (entry phase); corners labeled d (top-left), a (top-right), b (bottom-right), c (bottom-left).
- Find: $\Phi(t)$; EMF; current; its direction; the external force needed to keep $v$ constant.

**(1) Flux through the coil**
*Formulas:* penetration depth $vt$ ⇒ immersed area $l\cdot vt$.
*Calculations:* $\Phi = Blvt$.
**Answer:** **(c)** $\;vBlt$

**(2) EMF magnitude**
*Calculations:* $\mathcal{E} = \dfrac{d\Phi}{dt} = Blv$ — constant during entry.
**Answer:** **(b)** $\;vBl$

**(3) Induced current**
*Calculations:* $I = \dfrac{vBl}{R}$.
**Answer:** **(e)** $\;\dfrac{vBl}{R}$

**(4) Direction of the induced current**
*Formulas:* out-of-page flux increasing ⇒ Lenz wants into-page flux inside ⇒ **clockwise**.
*Calculations:* clockwise through the labels: a→b→c→d.
**Answer:** **(a)** $\;$a→b→c→d

**(5) External force for constant speed**
*Formulas:* the field drags the current-carrying entering side backward with $F = BIl$; match it.
*Calculations:* $F = \dfrac{vB^2l^2}{R}$.
**Answer:** **(d)** $\;\dfrac{vB^2l^2}{R}$
*Conclusion:* while the coil is *fully inside*, $\Phi$ is constant ⇒ $I = 0$ ⇒ $F = 0$ — a favorite follow-up; on exit everything repeats with reversed signs. Energy check: $Fv = \dfrac{v^2B^2l^2}{R} = I^2R$ ✓ — the pushing hand funds the Joule heat, exactly.

## Topic 43 — Snell's Law & Refraction

**Theory.** $n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2$, with angles measured **from the normal**. Bending *toward* the normal ⇒ entering a higher-$n$ (slower) medium; *away* ⇒ lower $n$. Inside a medium of index $n$: speed $c/n$, wavelength $\lambda/n$, frequency unchanged. **Sphere/droplet geometry:** the normal at the hit point is the radius, so a ray aimed with perpendicular offset $h$ at a sphere of radius $r$ meets the surface at incidence $\sin\theta_i = h/r$ — no protractor needed.

**Key Formulas**
- $n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2$ · sphere entry: $\sin\theta_i = \dfrac{h}{r}$ · $v = \dfrac{c}{n}$, $\;\lambda_n = \dfrac{\lambda}{n}$

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Draw the normal at the hit point. 2. Extract $\sin$(incidence) from geometry. 3. Snell across the boundary. 4. Through parallel layers, $n\sin\theta$ is invariant — jump layers freely.

#### Problem 2014-UG-Q1-(5) — Refraction into a water droplet
**Parameters**
- Given: spherical droplet of radius $r$, index $n$, center O; a horizontal ray strikes the surface at P, aimed with perpendicular offset $h$ from the line through O; refraction angle $\phi$ inside.
- Find: $\sin\phi$.
- Diagram: the normal at P lies along OP; the offset $h$ is the perpendicular distance from O to the incoming ray.

**Formulas needed:** $\sin\theta_i = \dfrac{h}{r}$; Snell: $1\cdot\sin\theta_i = n\sin\phi$.
**Calculations:** $\sin\phi = \dfrac{h}{nr}$.
**Answer:** **(f)** $\;\dfrac{h}{nr}$
**Conclusion:** the whole problem is *recognizing* $\sin\theta_i = h/r$. Limits: $h \to 0$ (central hit) ⇒ $\phi \to 0$ ✓; $h \to r$ (grazing) ⇒ $\sin\phi \to 1/n$ — exactly the critical-angle sine, a beautiful consistency check.

#### Problem 2015-UG-Q1-(5) — Comparing indices across an interface
**Parameters**
- Given: a ray crosses from a slab of index $n_1$ into a slab of index $n_2$; the printed figure shows the ray bending **away from the normal** in $n_2$ (the angle to the normal grows).
- Find: the relation between $n_1$ and $n_2$.

**Formulas needed:** $n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2$.
**Calculations:** bending away ⇒ $\theta_2 > \theta_1$ ⇒ $\sin\theta_2 > \sin\theta_1$ ⇒ $n_2 < n_1$.
**Answer:** **(a)** $\;n_1 > n_2$
**Conclusion:** a pure figure-reading question — **confirm the bend direction on your own printed copy** (a toward-the-normal bend flips the answer). One line to rehearse: *away from the normal = faster medium = smaller n*.

## Topic 44 — Total Internal Reflection

**Theory.** TIR happens only going from **high $n$ to low $n$**; critical angle $\sin\theta_c = \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$; beyond it, 100% reflection — the optical-fiber principle. **The waveguide complement trick:** if a ray inside the slab makes angle $\phi$ with the slab **axis** (i.e. with the entry-face normal), its incidence on the *walls* is $90°-\phi$, so the TIR condition becomes $\cos\phi > \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$. Zigzag bookkeeping: over an axial length $L$, the geometric path is $L/\cos\phi$, traveled at speed $c/n_1$.

**Key Formulas**
- $\sin\theta_c = \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$ ($n_1 > n_2$) · wall condition: $\cos\phi > \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$ · path $= \dfrac{L}{\cos\phi}$ · speed $= \dfrac{c}{n_1}$

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Snell at the entry face. 2. Complement the angle at the walls ($\sin \leftrightarrow \cos$). 3. Impose TIR. 4. Time = geometric path ÷ medium speed.

#### Problem 2016-UG-Q5 — Glass-slab waveguide (parts 1–4)
**Parameters (shared)**
- Given: core slab of index $n_1$ sandwiched by cladding $n_2$ above and below; a ray enters from vacuum at A on the end face with incidence $\theta$, refracting to angle $\phi$ from the axis; it zigzags by total internal reflection to B; the axial separation between the A and B faces is $L$; vacuum light speed $c$.
- Find: $\sin\phi$; the index condition enabling TIR; the angle condition on $\phi$; the travel time A→B.

**(1) Refraction at the entry face**
*Formulas:* Snell, vacuum side: $\sin\theta = n_1\sin\phi$.
*Calculations:* $\sin\phi = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{n_1}$.
**Answer:** **(c)** $\;\dfrac{\sin\theta}{n_1}$

**(2) Index condition for TIR at the walls**
*Formulas:* TIR requires the core denser than the cladding.
*Calculations:* $\dfrac{n_1}{n_2} > 1$.
**Answer:** **(c)** $\;\dfrac{n_1}{n_2} > 1$

**(3) Angle condition for guided propagation**
*Formulas:* wall incidence $= 90° - \phi$; TIR: $\sin(90°-\phi) > \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$.
*Calculations:* $\cos\phi > \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$.
**Answer:** **(e)** $\;\cos\phi > \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}$
*Conclusion:* the complement step ($\sin \to \cos$) is where the marks are lost. Physically this is why fibers accept only a cone of entry angles — the numerical aperture.

**(4) Travel time from A to B**
*Formulas:* fold the zigzag flat: total path $= \dfrac{L}{\cos\phi}$, at speed $\dfrac{c}{n_1}$.
*Calculations:* $t = \dfrac{L n_1}{c\cos\phi}$.
**Answer:** **(a)** $\;\dfrac{Ln_1}{c\cos\phi}$
*Conclusion:* the number of bounces never matters — reflections don't change the axial-progress bookkeeping. Limits: $\phi \to 0$ ⇒ $t \to Ln_1/c$, the straight-shot time ✓.

## Topic 45 — Interference: Young's Double Slit & Thin Films

**Theory (double slit).** Path difference at small angle: $d\sin\theta \approx \dfrac{dy}{L}$. **Bright:** $= m\lambda$; **dark:** $= \left(m + \frac{1}{2}\right)\lambda$, $m = 0, 1, 2, \dots$; fringe spacing $\Delta y = \dfrac{\lambda L}{d}$. The $k$-th dark line from the center sits at $y = \left(k - \frac{1}{2}\right)\dfrac{\lambda L}{d}$ — darks at $\frac{1}{2}, \frac{3}{2}, \frac{5}{2}, \dots$ in units of $\lambda L/d$.

**Theory (thin film, thickness $t$, index $n$, near-normal) [theory only in this set].** Optical path difference between the two reflections: $2nt$, **plus a half-wave flip at any reflection off a higher-$n$ medium**. With exactly one flip (air–film–air): bright ⇔ $2nt = \left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)\lambda$, dark ⇔ $2nt = m\lambda$. With zero or two flips, the conditions swap. Count the flips first, always.

**Problem-Solving Framework**
1. Write the path difference. 2. Choose bright/dark condition (films: count phase flips). 3. **Count the fringes out loud** — the first dark is $m = 0$ (half-integer $\frac{1}{2}$), the third is $\frac{5}{2}$.

#### Problem 2018-UG-Q1-(4) — Position of the third dark fringe
**Parameters**
- Given: slit spacing $d$, screen distance $L$ (large), wavelength $\lambda$; O = central bright spot.
- Find: distance from O to the **third** dark line.

**Formulas needed:** dark: $\dfrac{dy}{L} = \left(m + \frac{1}{2}\right)\lambda$; third dark ⇒ $m = 2$ ⇒ path difference $\frac{5}{2}\lambda$.
**Calculations:** $y = \dfrac{5\lambda L}{2d}$.
**Answer:** **(f)** $\;\dfrac{5L\lambda}{2d}$
**Conclusion:** counting is the entire game: darks at $\frac{1}{2}, \frac{3}{2}, \frac{5}{2}$ — "third" means $\frac{5}{2}$, not 3. The option $\frac{3\lambda L}{2d}$ is the *second* dark (one miscount), and $\frac{3\lambda L}{d}$ is the third **bright**. Recite the ladder before answering.

## Topic 46 — Light Path Through Multiple Media

**Theory.** Across any stack of **parallel** interfaces, $n\sin\theta$ is conserved at every boundary (the *Snell invariant*) — so the exit ray into the original medium is **parallel to the entry ray** (laterally shifted only), and you may jump directly between any two layers: $n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_3\sin\theta_3$ without solving the middle layer. Within each layer: speed $c/n$, wavelength $\lambda/n$, frequency fixed.

**Worked variants:** the two-slab comparison 2015-Q1-(5) (Topic 43); the time-cost of travel inside a medium, 2016-Q5-(4) (Topic 44): $t \propto n \times$ geometric path.
